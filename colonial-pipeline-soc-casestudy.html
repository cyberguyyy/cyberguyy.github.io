Chapter One

Background & Threat Actor Profile

Understanding who attacked, why, and what made Colonial Pipeline an irresistible target for DarkSide ransomware-as-a-service operators.

The Target

Colonial Pipeline Company

Operator of the largest refined petroleum pipeline in the United States. Runs 5,500 miles from Houston, TX to Linden, NJ — supplying jet fuel, diesel, and gasoline to the Southeast and East Coast. Processes 2.5 million barrels daily. A single point of failure for critical national infrastructure.

Why It Was Targeted

The Attacker's Logic

  • Critical infrastructure = maximum pressure to pay quickly
  • OT/IT convergence = attackers found IT network, company feared OT spillover
  • Legacy systems = older industrial networks, poor segmentation
  • High revenue = ability to pay large ransoms
  • Reputational risk = public company, heavily regulated
Threat Actor — DarkSide

Who Is DarkSide?

DarkSide is a Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) operation believed to be based in Eastern Europe (likely Russia). First appeared in August 2020. Operated a professional criminal enterprise with:

  • Developer team — built and maintained the ransomware
  • Affiliates — independent hackers who deploy it for 20–30% cut
  • Leak site — "DarkSide Leaks" on Tor for double extortion
  • PR operation — claimed to donate to charity, avoid hospitals
  • Avoided CIS countries — malware checks system locale, won't run if Russian/Ukrainian
DarkSide Technical Profile

Malware Characteristics

  • Written in: C++ (Windows), Go (Linux/ESXi variant)
  • Encryption: RSA-1024 + Salsa20 (custom implementation)
  • Speed: Fast — partial file encryption technique
  • Self-deletion: Deletes itself post-execution
  • VSS wiper: Clears shadow copies automatically
  • Config-driven: Affiliates customize targets, ransom amount
⚠️

The Root Cause — One Compromised Password

FBI investigation confirmed initial access was gained through a single compromised VPN account — a legacy VPN that did not have multi-factor authentication enabled. The password was found in a batch of leaked credentials on the dark web. The account was no longer in active use, but was never deprovisioned. This single oversight enabled a $4.4M ransom, 6-day national emergency, and a historic crisis in U.S. fuel supply.

Security Posture (Pre-Attack)

Known Weaknesses

  • Legacy VPN without MFA
  • Stale accounts not deprovisioned
  • Poor IT/OT network segmentation
  • Limited visibility on OT network
  • No 24/7 SOC monitoring
Pre-Compromise Intelligence

Dark Web Activity

  • Credential dump containing Colonial accounts circulated before attack
  • DarkSide affiliate recruited for "oil & gas" target weeks prior
  • No threat intel team monitoring dark web mentions
  • No automated credential monitoring service deployed
Regulatory Context

Compliance vs. Security

  • TSA regulated pipeline cybersecurity loosely pre-2021
  • No mandatory incident reporting requirement at time
  • Post-attack: TSA issued emergency cybersecurity directives
  • Exposed gap between "compliant" and "secure"

Attack Timeline — Hour by Hour

A reconstruction of events from initial credential use through ransom payment and recovery. Click each event to expand details.

🕐

Attacker Dwell Time: Approximately 9 Days

DarkSide affiliates first accessed Colonial Pipeline's network around April 29, 2021. Encryption began May 7, 2021 — giving attackers nearly 9 days to map the environment, steal data, and position for maximum impact before triggering ransomware. This is the window where detection must happen.

Pre-Encryption Phase (Attacker Dwell)

~April 29, 2021 · Day 0
Initial VPN Access
Attacker authenticates to legacy VPN using stolen credentials found in a dark web credential dump. No MFA. Access is logged but no alert fires.
What happened: A VPN account associated with a former or inactive user was compromised. The credentials appeared in a batch of leaked passwords — likely from an unrelated breach. The attacker connected outside normal business hours from an Eastern European IP. No geolocation-based alerts were configured. The VPN logs show successful authentication, but no SIEM correlation rule fired because the account was technically valid.

What should have fired: Impossible travel / new geolocation alert. Inactive account login alert. After-hours VPN connection from new IP.
April 29 – May 5 · Days 1–7
Internal Reconnaissance
Attackers enumerate AD, map file shares, identify backup servers and billing systems. Tools: BloodHound, ADFind, net commands.
What happened: Over multiple sessions, attackers ran BloodHound/SharpHound to map Active Directory structure and identify privileged accounts and attack paths. ADFind queries enumerated groups, OUs, and domain controllers. Net commands (net view, net user, net group) gathered host and user information. The internal recon traffic blended with normal IT admin activity.

What should have fired: BloodHound collection artifact detection (LDAP queries at scale), excessive SMB enumeration from non-admin workstation, unusual net.exe usage from VPN-sourced IP.
May 4–6 · Days 5–7
Credential Harvesting & Lateral Movement
Mimikatz used to dump LSASS credentials. Pass-the-Hash and Pass-the-Ticket attacks pivot attacker to privileged accounts including domain admin.
What happened: Attackers used Mimikatz to extract NTLM hashes and Kerberos tickets from LSASS memory. With harvested credentials, they performed Pass-the-Hash attacks to move laterally across servers without needing plaintext passwords. Eventually compromised a Domain Admin account, giving full control of the Windows environment.

What should have fired: LSASS memory access by non-system process, anomalous NTLM authentication patterns, new admin logins from VPN IP, Kerberoasting detection (unusual SPN requests).
May 6 · Day 8
Data Exfiltration (~100GB)
Attackers exfiltrate approximately 100GB of data to a cloud storage endpoint before triggering encryption. This enables double extortion.
What happened: Using a cloud-based exfiltration tool (believed to be Rclone or similar), attackers compressed and uploaded approximately 100GB of sensitive data — billing information, contracts, business data — to a DarkSide-controlled cloud endpoint. The transfer completed over several hours. This data later appeared on DarkSide's Tor leak site as leverage.

What should have fired: Large outbound data transfer to non-corporate endpoint, unusual process accessing multiple file shares rapidly, cloud upload tool (rclone.exe) execution, DLP alert on bulk file copy outside business hours.

Detection & Response Phase

May 7, 2021 · ~05:00 AM ET
Ransomware Deployed — Encryption Begins
DarkSide ransomware executed across IT network. VSS deleted, services stopped. Ransom note dropped. Billing system encrypted — operations continue briefly but invoice/flow data unavailable.
What happened: The affiliate executed DarkSide ransomware using domain admin credentials via PsExec/WMI across multiple systems simultaneously. The malware first deleted Volume Shadow Copies, stopped backup services, then began encrypting files with a .darkside extension. A ransom note "README.[ext].TXT" appeared in every folder. The billing and flow allocation systems were primary targets — encrypting them made it impossible to safely bill customers for fuel delivery quantities.
May 7 · ~06:00 AM ET
Ransomware Note Discovered by Operator
A Colonial employee discovers ransom note on their system. IT notified. Incident response begins. FireEye (Mandiant) engaged as external IR firm.
What happened: An employee attempting to access a work file found a ransom note instead. IT was alerted, who quickly confirmed widespread encryption across the corporate network. The CISO and executive leadership were notified within minutes. The decision was made to shut down pipeline operations as a precautionary measure — fearing ransomware could spread to OT systems controlling the physical pipeline. Mandiant (FireEye) was called immediately as the retained IR firm.
May 7 · ~09:00 AM ET
Pipeline Operations Halted
Colonial shuts down 5,500-mile pipeline proactively. Not confirmed that OT systems were compromised — but IT/OT boundary was unclear. Safety-first decision.
What happened: Out of caution — and critically, because their billing systems were encrypted, making it impossible to safely know how much fuel was flowing to whom — Colonial shut down the entire pipeline. The lack of visibility into the OT/ICS environment meant they couldn't verify whether SCADA/DCS systems were infected. The shutdown was NOT because OT was compromised, but because they lacked confidence to operate safely without IT systems. This decision caused immediate national fuel shortage.
May 7–8
FBI Notified, CISA Engaged, Negotiations Begin
Colonial notifies FBI. CISA issues emergency directive. National emergency declared. Colonial contacts DarkSide's negotiation portal — ransom discussions begin.
Ransom negotiation: DarkSide's initial demand was reported at $5M. Colonial's negotiators (via a specialist firm) engaged through the dark web chat interface provided in the ransom note. DarkSide provided a "proof of decryption" — decrypting a sample file to demonstrate they held the key. Colonial ultimately paid approximately $4.4M in Bitcoin within hours of the attack. The decryption tool provided was slow and inadequate — Colonial also relied on their own backups for recovery.
May 12, 2021
Pipeline Operations Resume
Full pipeline operations restored 6 days after shutdown. Fuel shortages and panic buying caused gas station outages across 11 states. Avg gas price hit $3/gallon for first time since 2014.
Recovery path: Systems were restored using a combination of the DarkSide decryptor (slow, used for critical files) and restored backups. The FBI recovered $2.3M of the ransom in June 2021 by seizing a Bitcoin wallet — the private key was somehow obtained. DarkSide went dark shortly after the attack — apparently under pressure from Russian authorities concerned about the diplomatic fallout.
June 7, 2021
DOJ Seizes $2.3M in Ransom Bitcoin
FBI's Virtual Asset Exploitation Unit traces and seizes approximately 63.7 BTC (~$2.3M) from a wallet controlled by a DarkSide affiliate — demonstrating that crypto is NOT fully anonymous.
How they did it: The FBI traced the Bitcoin payments through the blockchain. The specific wallet address used to receive the ransom was identified. The DOJ obtained a court order to seize the funds. The private key for the wallet was obtained — the exact method was not fully disclosed, but it's believed the wallet was hosted on a U.S.-based crypto exchange, giving FBI legal jurisdiction. This recovery was unprecedented at the time.

Chapter Three

SIEM Detection — What Should Have Fired

A deep dive into the SIEM alerts that were either missed, absent, or not acted on — and the exact detection logic that could have caught DarkSide days before encryption.

🔴

SIEM Gap: 9 Days of Undetected Activity

The DarkSide affiliate operated inside Colonial's network for approximately 9 days before encryption. A properly tuned SIEM should have generated high-fidelity alerts within hours of initial access. Below are the specific detection opportunities and the Splunk/Sigma rules that would catch them.

ALERT 1 — VPN Access from New Geolocation (Missed)

The very first thing that should have triggered: a successful VPN login from a new country/IP that had never previously authenticated. This is a trivial detection to implement.

⚡

Expected Alert: Suspicious VPN Authentication

Severity: HIGH · Category: Initial Access · Rule: New Geolocation + Inactive Account + After Hours

# Actual VPN Authentication Log (reconstructed) 2021-04-29 03:14:22 UTC vpn-gateway-01.colonial.local auth.log event=VPN_LOGIN user=svc_colonial_legacy src_ip=185.220.101.XX country=UA last_login=2019-11-14 (543 days ago) mfa_used=false ⚠ NO ALERT GENERATED — Rule not configured
## Splunk SPL — VPN Impossible Travel / New Geolocation Detection index=network_auth sourcetype=vpn_logs action=success | eval login_country=iplocation(src_ip) | stats values(login_country) as countries dc(login_country) as country_count earliest(_time) as first_seen latest(_time) as last_seen by user | where country_count > 1 AND last_seen - first_seen < 86400 | eval alert="IMPOSSIBLE_TRAVEL: User logged in from multiple countries within 24hrs" --- ## Also: Alert on inactive accounts (no login in 90+ days) index=network_auth sourcetype=vpn_logs action=success | join user [ search index=ad_logs | where datediff(now(), last_logon_date) > 90 | fields user ] | eval alert="DORMANT_ACCOUNT_VPN_LOGIN: Account inactive 90+ days just authenticated"

ALERT 2 — AD Enumeration / BloodHound Collection (Missed)

BloodHound's SharpHound collector generates massive volumes of LDAP queries in a short time — a very detectable pattern. Thousands of LDAP requests from a workstation in seconds is not normal IT behavior.

## Sigma Rule — BloodHound/SharpHound Collection title: Possible BloodHound AD Enumeration status: stable logsource: product: windows service: security detection: selection: EventID: 4661 ObjectType: - 'SAM_USER' - 'SAM_GROUP' - 'SAM_DOMAIN' timeframe: 10s condition: selection | count() by SubjectUserName > 200 level: high tags: - attack.discovery - attack.t1087

ALERT 3 — LSASS Memory Access (Should Have Fired)

# EDR/Sysmon Event — LSASS Access (Mimikatz) 2021-05-05 01:44:12 WRK-FIN-08.colonial.local EventID=10 (Sysmon: ProcessAccess) SourceImage: C:\Users\Public\Temp\srv.exe TargetImage: C:\Windows\System32\lsass.exe GrantedAccess: 0x1010 (PROCESS_VM_READ) CallTrace: srv.exe+0x2341f ⚠ Non-system process accessing LSASS memory # This IS a high-confidence Mimikatz indicator
## Splunk — LSASS Memory Access by Suspicious Process index=endpoint EventCode=10 TargetImage="*lsass.exe" NOT SourceImage IN ( "*MsMpEng.exe", /* Defender */ "*svchost.exe", /* System */ "*csrss.exe", /* System */ "*lsm.exe" /* System */ ) GrantedAccess IN ("0x1010", "0x1410", "0x143a") | eval severity="CRITICAL" | eval alert="CREDENTIAL_DUMP: Suspected Mimikatz"

ALERT 4 — VSS Deletion: Pre-Encryption Canary (Critical Miss)

VSS deletion is one of the most reliable pre-ransomware signals that exists. ANY instance of vssadmin deleting shadows should trigger an immediate high-priority alert with auto-isolation. There is no legitimate admin reason to do this outside of a planned, documented maintenance window.

# Process Creation Log — VSS Deletion 2021-05-07 04:58:33 FIN-SVR-01.colonial.local EventID=4688 NewProcessName: C:\Windows\System32\vssadmin.exe CommandLine: vssadmin.exe delete shadows /all /quiet ParentProcess: C:\Windows\System32\cmd.exe User: COLONIAL\svc_admin_legacy (DA) 2021-05-07 04:58:34 CommandLine: wbadmin delete catalog -quiet 2021-05-07 04:58:35 CommandLine: bcdedit /set {default} recoveryenabled No ⚠⚠ PRE-RANSOMWARE INDICATOR SEQUENCE DETECTED ⚠⚠ ENCRYPTION WILL BEGIN IN <60 SECONDS
## Splunk — VSS Deletion + Recovery Disable Chain index=endpoint EventCode=4688 ( CommandLine="*vssadmin* delete*shadows*" OR CommandLine="*wmic* shadowcopy* delete*" OR CommandLine="*wbadmin* delete*catalog*" OR CommandLine="*bcdedit* recoveryenabled*no*" OR CommandLine="*bcdedit* safeboot*" ) | stats count values(CommandLine) as cmds dc(host) as hosts by user, _time span=5m | where count >= 2 | eval severity="CRITICAL" | eval action="AUTO_ISOLATE + PAGE_SOC_IMMEDIATELY" --- ## Recommendation: This rule should trigger ## automated endpoint isolation via SOAR/EDR API ## DO NOT wait for analyst — time is seconds

ALERT 5 — Mass File Encryption in Progress (Last Resort Detection)

# FileSystem Event Logs — Mass Rename (Honeypot / File Integrity) 2021-05-07 05:01:14 FIN-SVR-01 File renamed: Q3_Report.xlsx → Q3_Report.xlsx.darkside (1 of 847 in 12 seconds) 2021-05-07 05:01:14 FIN-SVR-02 File renamed: payroll_2021.csv → payroll_2021.csv.darkside (1 of 1,203 in 15 seconds) 2021-05-07 05:01:15 FILE-SVR-01 Ransom note created: README.darkside.TXT in 847 directories simultaneously ⚠ RANSOMWARE CONFIRMED — ENCRYPTION ACTIVE ACROSS 3+ HOSTS # At this point, isolation is the ONLY response. Recovery depends on backups. # Detection here = too late to prevent encryption. Earlier alerts are critical.
Detection Opportunity 1

⏱ Day 1 — VPN Alert

Dormant account + new geo + no MFA. Would have stopped the attack at entry. Easiest to implement. Highest value.

Detection Opportunity 2

⏱ Day 2 — Recon Alert

BloodHound LDAP burst pattern. Would have given 8 days to respond before encryption. Clear behavioral signal.

Detection Opportunity 3

⏱ Day 9 — VSS Deletion

Last chance before encryption. Automated isolation could have stopped spread. Minutes matter — this needs SOAR auto-response.

Triggered Indicators (If EDR Was Active)

  • SUSPICIOUS_PROCESS_CHAIN: wmic spawning PowerShell with encoded args
  • LSASS_ACCESS: srv.exe (unsigned) accessing lsass.exe memory

  • AV_KILL: Process terminating security service via net stop
  • UNSIGNED_BINARY: Executable in C:\Users\Public\ — no valid signature
DarkSide Binary Analysis

Malware Characteristics (EDR Telemetry)

  • File: srv.exe (disguised as service binary)
  • Hash (SHA256): 9cee5522a7ca2bfca7cd3d9daba23e9a30deb6205f56c12045839075f7627297
  • Compiler: MSVC (C++), no debug symbols

  • Import table: CryptGenKey, CryptEncrypt, CreateFileW, FindFirstFileW

EDR Response Capability

What Modern EDR Can Do Automatically

  • Kill malicious process immediately upon ransomware behavior detection
  • Isolate endpoint from network while maintaining EDR comms
  • Rollback encrypted files (SentinelOne Rollback, CrowdStrike File Recovery)
  • Block hash across all enterprise endpoints instantly
  • Preserve memory dump for forensics before isolation
  • Alert SIEM + SOAR for orchestrated response
Colonial's EDR Gap

What Was Missing

  • EDR coverage was not enterprise-wide — IT/OT boundary endpoints unmonitored
  • EDR policies in detect-only mode, not prevent/isolate mode
  • No 24/7 SOC watching EDR console — alerts sat unactioned overnight
  • No SOAR integration — manual analyst process required for response
  • Legacy systems with no EDR agent installed

MITRE ATT&CK Framework Mapping

Complete mapping of every observed DarkSide technique to MITRE ATT&CK — the universal language for describing adversary behavior used in threat intel, detection engineering, and IR reports.

ℹ️

What is MITRE ATT&CK?

MITRE ATT&CK is a globally-accessible knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques based on real-world observations. As a SOC analyst, mapping incidents to ATT&CK helps you communicate findings clearly, write better detection rules, and understand where your defensive gaps are. Every technique has a unique ID (T####) you'll see in threat intel reports, SIEM rule sets, and vendor advisories.

The Golden Technique

T1490 (Inhibit System Recovery) is the single highest-value detection for ransomware. Every major ransomware family runs VSS deletion. If you have ONE automated response rule, make it this. Alert + auto-isolate on any vssadmin delete shadows command, no exceptions.

Chapter Six

Incident Response Playbook

What the SOC should have done — and what Colonial's IR team actually did. A step-by-step L1/L2/L3 response framework based on this incident.

📋

How to Read This Playbook

L1 (SOC Analyst L1): First responder — triage, basic containment, escalation. L2 (SOC Analyst L2): Deep investigation, forensics, threat hunting. L3/IR (Incident Responder / IR Lead): Full incident command, external engagement, recovery decisions.

PHASE 1 — DETECTION & TRIAGE

1
L1 ANALYST — IMMEDIATE (0–5 min)

Acknowledge & Classify the Alert

Alert fires in SIEM (VSS deletion, mass file rename, or ransomware note detected). L1 opens ticket in TheHive/ServiceNow. Classifies as P1 — Ransomware. Does NOT close or snooze. Immediately notifies L2 on-call via Slack/phone.

2
L1 ANALYST — 5–15 min

Initial Scope Assessment

Determine: How many hosts are affected? Is this one endpoint or spreading across the network? Check SIEM for correlated alerts on other hosts. Look for ransom notes on file servers. Identify source host (patient zero). DO NOT reboot or run antivirus scans on affected hosts — this destroys evidence.

3
L1 ANALYST — 10–15 min

Network Isolation of Affected Endpoints

Use EDR console (CrowdStrike: Contain Host / SentinelOne: Disconnect from Network) to isolate affected endpoints from the network while maintaining EDR communication. If EDR is unavailable: disable NIC, change VLAN, or have network team block switch port. Document every action with timestamp.

PHASE 2 — INVESTIGATION (L2 LEAD)

4
L2 ANALYST — 0–30 min after escalation

Identify Patient Zero & Initial Access Vector

Query SIEM for the earliest evidence of attacker activity. Look for: first occurrence of malicious process, first authentication from attacker IP, first anomalous network connection. Build a timeline. In Colonial's case: query VPN logs for the compromised account, trace back to first login from foreign IP 9 days prior.

# Splunk query to find patient zero index=endpoint host=* srv.exe OR darkside.exe OR *.darkside | stats min(_time) as first_seen by host | sort first_seen asc | head 1 # Result: FIN-SVR-01 at 2021-05-07 04:57:11 → Patient Zero
5
L2 ANALYST — 30–90 min

Evidence Collection & Preservation

Before reimaging ANYTHING: collect memory dump (Volatility/Magnet RAM Capture), disk image (write-blocked), all relevant logs (Windows Event Logs, Sysmon, EDR telemetry, VPN logs, firewall logs). Chain of custody documentation is mandatory if law enforcement involvement is anticipated. Contact FBI immediately — they need notification within hours.

6
L2 ANALYST — 1–3 hours

Full Scope & Blast Radius Assessment

How many systems encrypted? Are backups affected? Is OT/ICS network reachable from IT? Are domain controllers compromised? If DA is compromised: entire AD environment is untrusted and must be rebuilt. Check for data exfiltration evidence — Rclone, large outbound transfers, cloud uploads. This determines whether double extortion applies.

7
L2 ANALYST — Ongoing

MITRE ATT&CK Mapping & TTP Documentation

Document every observed technique with evidence. Map to ATT&CK. This feeds into: detection rule improvements, threat intel sharing (ISAC), legal documentation, insurance claims, and the post-incident report. Use ATT&CK Navigator to build a visual coverage map and show management where gaps exist.

PHASE 3 — CONTAINMENT & RECOVERY (IR LEAD)

8
IR LEAD — Hours 2–6

Executive & Legal Notification

CISO, CTO, CEO notified. Legal counsel engaged — attorney-client privilege applied to IR findings. Cyber insurance carrier notified (most policies require timely notification). Regulatory notifications assessed (TSA for pipeline operators, SEC for public companies, HHS for healthcare). PR team on standby for potential public disclosure.

9
IR LEAD — Hours 4–12

Ransom Decision (Pay vs. Don't Pay)

Critical decision tree: Are offline backups available and clean? How long will recovery take from backups vs. paying? What is the business impact per day of downtime? Is OFAC compliance required (paying sanctioned actors is illegal)? Colonial paid because backup restoration would take longer than the business could tolerate — and they feared OT compromise. FBI typically advises against paying.

Rebuild from known-good images — do NOT restore encrypted systems. Rebuild domain controllers first. Reset ALL credentials (assume all passwords compromised if DA was hit). Reimage affected endpoints. Restore data from offline/immutable backups. Verify backup integrity before restoration. Chapter Seven

Lessons Learned — SOC Analyst Takeaways

Every major ransomware incident teaches the security community something. These are the enduring lessons from Colonial Pipeline for every SOC analyst and future SOP developer.

🔑 Lesson 1: One Password = National Emergency

A single compromised credential in a dark web dump, combined with no MFA and no account lifecycle management, enabled a $4.4M ransom and a 6-day national emergency. Identity hygiene is not a checkbox — it's the most critical control in your environment.

FIX: MFA everywhere. Credential monitoring service (SpyCloud, Have I Been Pwned Enterprise). Automated account deprovisioning. Privileged access management (PAM).

👁️ Lesson 2: 9 Days of Invisible Activity

The attacker had 9 days inside the network generating BloodHound queries, dumping credentials, and staging exfiltration. A properly configured SIEM should have caught this within hours. Detection latency directly determines blast radius.

FIX: Tune SIEM for behavioral detections. Enable Sysmon on all endpoints. 24/7 SOC or MDR coverage. Threat hunting schedule targeting dwell-period TTPs.

🔒 Lesson 3: IT/OT Convergence Is Your Biggest Risk

Colonial shut down physical pipeline operations NOT because OT was compromised — but because IT was, and they couldn't see the OT network clearly enough to know. Poor IT/OT segmentation created maximum uncertainty during a crisis.

FIX: Implement proper DMZ between IT and OT. Deploy OT-specific security monitoring (Claroty, Dragos, Nozomi). Document all communication paths between IT and OT networks.

💾 Lesson 4: Backups Are Your Last Line of Defense

Colonial's decryptor from DarkSide was slow and incomplete. Their fastest recovery path was restoring from backups. But many organizations discover their backups are inadequate, not isolated, or also encrypted when ransomware hits — too late.

FIX: 3-2-1-1 backup rule. Offline/air-gapped copy. Immutable cloud backups. Test restoration quarterly — untested backups are not backups.

🚨 Lesson 5: VSS Deletion Is Your Last Chance Before Encryption

Every single ransomware family deletes shadow copies before encrypting. This is a universal, highly reliable indicator. Detecting and auto-isolating on VSS deletion — before encryption completes — is the most impactful single detection rule you can write.

FIX: SOAR playbook: vssadmin delete shadows → immediate auto-isolation via EDR API + alert + page on-call. No analyst intervention required for speed.

🌐 Lesson 6: Crypto Isn't Anonymous

The FBI recovered $2.3M of Colonial's $4.4M ransom by tracing Bitcoin through the blockchain and seizing a wallet. This was the first major ransomware ransom recovery and changed the calculus for threat actors — now many use privacy coins (Monero) specifically to avoid this.

IMPLICATION: Work with FBI immediately. Blockchain tracing is a real capability. Never make ransom payments without FBI awareness — they may be able to help recover funds.
